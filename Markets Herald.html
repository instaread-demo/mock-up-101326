Jamie Horowitz and Peyton Manning’s Omaha Productions Evolution: Not Just Sports

When Peyton Manning and Jamie Horowitz began building Omaha Productions, the company’s connection to sports was obvious. Manning was one of the most famous quarterbacks in NFL history, while Horowitz had been president of Fox Sports 1 and, before that, had run and reinvented ESPN’s original programming.

Six years later, sports may still be at the heart of Omaha Productions. But increasingly, it doesn’t define the boundaries of the company.

The latest example came this week, when Roku announced Battle of the Streaming Stars, a new Omaha Productions competition series hosted and executive produced by Terry Crews. A reimagining of the classic Battle of the Network Stars, the show will bring together comedians, actors, reality stars and digital creators to compete in a series of challenges. The first of two hour-long specials premieres October 23 on The Roku Channel.

The premise feels like Omaha — competition, humor, recognizable personalities and a format designed to bring people together — but notably, it isn’t really a sports show.

And that may be the bigger story.

Over the past several years, Manning and Horowitz have quietly been expanding Omaha from a sports-focused production company into a broader entertainment business, taking the sensibility that helped make ManningCast and Quarterback successful and applying it to scripted television, reality competition, music, professional wrestling, collectibles, branded entertainment, history and other areas of popular culture.

The strategy isn’t to move away from sports. It is to use what Omaha learned from sports to move into everything else.

“Sports will always be part of who we are, but it doesn’t define what Omaha can be,” said Horowitz. “If something is uplifting, unifying and brings people together, we believe it can come from Omaha.”

The expansion beyond sports has actually been underway for some time. Omaha partnered with LL COOL J on Hip Hop Was Born Here, a Paramount+ docuseries exploring the people and places that shaped hip-hop. In country music, the company produced Hulu’s It’s All Country, hosted by Luke Bryan, which explored the stories behind some of the genre’s most important songs and artists. And Omaha has moved into history programming with The Mega-Brands That Built America, the History Channel series exploring the origins of some of the country’s most recognizable companies.

For Manning, those projects are not as far removed from football as they might initially appear. The common thread is curiosity — about people, competition, culture and how successful things get built.

“I love being a part of shows that are outside of sports,” Manning told Forbes while discussing Mega-Brands. “One of the core tenants at Omaha Productions is that we want to champion hard work.”

That willingness to move beyond the obvious is increasingly becoming part of Omaha’s identity. Sports remains its foundation, but it is no longer the only lens through which Manning and Horowitz are looking for stories.

Manning made a similar connection when Omaha ventured into hip-hop.

“Music is far from my area of expertise, but sports and music have a lot of similarities,” Manning said when Hip Hop Was Born Here was announced. “Both require hard work and commitment, and at their best, both bring people together.”

Perhaps the clearest sign of that evolution came with Chad Powers. The Hulu comedy, starring Glen Powell, was inspired by the viral undercover character created by Eli Manning on Omaha-produced Eli’s Places. The series represented Omaha’s move into scripted entertainment. Football provided the seed of the idea, but the finished product was something entirely different: a scripted comedy designed to reach an audience well beyond traditional sports fans.

Professional wrestling has provided another bridge. Omaha’s WWE: Unreal took viewers behind the scenes of WWE, focusing not simply on what happened in the ring but on the creative process, personalities and decisions that shape the entertainment product. Like many of Omaha’s projects, the attraction was less about a particular sport than about gaining unusual access to an interesting world and the people who inhabit it.

Even some of Omaha’s projects that begin squarely in sports have demonstrated how the company’s ambitions have expanded.

Quarterback, Receiver, Starting 5 and Full Court Press use sports as their setting, but their storytelling is often about family, pressure, ambition, relationships and what happens away from the field or court. That approach has helped turn athletes into characters audiences can follow whether or not they care deeply about the final score.

It also reflects a philosophy that has been part of Omaha since Manning and Horowitz started working together. The company’s stated mission is to bring people together by making uplifting and unifying content. That mission is intentionally broader than sports.

The same expansion can be seen on the business side.

Omaha Brand Studio has worked with major companies including Macy’s, FanDuel, Nestlé, U.S. Bank, Nationwide, Topps, Toyota and the American Red Cross, creating campaigns that increasingly look less like traditional athlete endorsements and more like entertainment. Omaha has also worked with athletes including Saquon Barkley, Baker Mayfield and Christian McCaffrey to help build content around their personalities.

For Horowitz, that reflects an important distinction in what Omaha is trying to become. The goal isn’t simply to produce more television shows. It is to build a company that can work with talent, brands and distribution partners across different formats and different parts of culture.

“We say in team meetings all the time that our partners matter most,” explained Horowitz. “We aren’t chasing margins. We want to make great things and make our teammates look good. We want to be in the long-term relationship business.”

That diversification has become increasingly important across the entertainment industry, where traditional television economics have changed and production companies have been forced to think more broadly about where content lives and how it gets financed.

Omaha has responded by moving in several directions at once: premium unscripted series, live television, podcasts, digital content, branded entertainment, scripted programming and now entertainment formats like Battle of the Streaming Stars.

At the same time, Manning and Horowitz have continued investing heavily in the sports programming that originally established the company.

ManningCast remains one of ESPN’s most distinctive NFL franchises. Omaha has built a growing collection of football programming and podcasts around voices including Mina Kimes, Peter Schrager and Kevin Clark. Its Places franchise has expanded across sports. And the company’s documentary and unscripted slate continues to rely on the access and credibility Manning can bring to athletes and leagues.

In other words, Omaha isn’t trying to become less of a sports company.

It is trying to become more than one.

That distinction helps explain why Battle of the Streaming Stars is an interesting next step. The show borrows one of sports’ simplest ideas — put people into competition and see what happens — but populates that world with entertainers and creators. Terry Crews brings his own combination of sports and entertainment experience, while the format itself is built around personalities, humor and competition rather than athletic excellence.

It also fits naturally with the culture Manning and Horowitz have tried to build inside Omaha. The company’s offices in New York, Los Angeles and Charlotte display the same simple sign: “Work Hard and Be Nice to People.” It is a simple philosophy that Horowitz focuses on and one that has become connective tissue for Omaha employees as the company has grown.

The company’s growth has also attracted significant outside investment, including backing connected to Patrick Whitesell and Silver Lake, as Omaha has reached a valuation reportedly north of $750 million.

But the more revealing measure of Omaha’s evolution may be the range of projects now carrying its name.

A Peyton Manning company producing football shows was always easy to understand.

A Peyton Manning and Jamie Horowitz company producing NFL broadcasts, Netflix docuseries, scripted comedies, hip-hop documentaries, country music programming, history shows, professional wrestling series, branded entertainment and a Roku competition show starring actors, comedians, reality personalities and creators is something different.

Six years into Omaha Productions, Manning’s football career still gives the company an unmistakable identity.

It just no longer gives it a boundary.
