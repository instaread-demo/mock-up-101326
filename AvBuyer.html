Flight Departments: Managing Passenger Expectation vs Reality

Aviation Director Andre Fodor shares his thoughts and experiences on managing passenger expectations, keeping business aircraft users grounded in operational realities…

Flight Departments
Andre Fodor  |  12 Oct 2026
    Back to Articles

    With a focused approach on global excellence and creativity, Andre Fodor has managed flight operations for the U.N. and Flight Options as well as being a senior demonstration pilot and instructor for Embraer Aircraft.  He is currently the Director of Aviation and Chief Pilot for Johnsonville...

    Serving as pilots and aviation managers, the crucial challenge is to deliver on the expectations of our principals. Over a thirty year career as a flight operations manager, I’ve come to learn that expectations and perceptions are, themselves, a form of reality.

    Unfortunately, expectations can also become over-inflated and unrealistic through overselling or wrong assumptions about the aircraft. While it’s only right that our principals should have high expectations of the machines they buy and the service they pay for, it is vital for those expectations to be tempered with an awareness of operational limitations, and the many nuances affecting how the service can feasibly be delivered.

    When I worked as an OEM demonstration pilot, I witnessed salespeople making broad, generous promises about an airplane’s capabilities. So, I spoke to the President of Sales and proposed a different strategy.

    Instead of delivering marketing performance (containing generalized performance factoids), why not deliver real-life, real-time, actual flight plan data and performance, tailored to the specific needs of the prospective buyer and airport pairs? We also changed the way we compared similar products.

    We utilized real-life scenarios and showcased what our airplanes could deliver instead of being critical of the competition. For serious prospects we proposed replicating the on-paper information in an actual flight if we felt it would help seal the deal.

    The new approach delivered new sales and – from some of the larger operators – fleet upgrade commitments. We generally found that aircraft owners had a happier experience with their new aircraft, enjoyed a deeper understanding of what could be reasonably expected of their acquisitions, and – sure enough – the satisfaction surveys improved. (We even made more sales through word-of-mouth.)

    Passenger Expectations: Being Real is Being Prepared

    While 100% dispatchability is simply not attainable in aviation – last-minute malfunctions, glitches, and mandatory maintenance will inevitably cause the aircraft to be out of service occasionally - Murphy’s Law dictates it’ll be exactly when your principal needs to fly.

    For the owners that demand and require such high reliability, being real about the prospect of occasional unavailability will enable a constructive exploration of alternative back-up options, whether (in the extreme) acquiring a second aircraft to provide redundancy, or (more commonly) having a Part 135 or Part 91K backup to provide a supplemental lift alternative when the owned jet is unavailable.

    Honest, open dialogue paves the path to real solutions rather than friction and disappointment. Aircraft owners who are used to a bespoke service will lose their feeling of operational control, scheduling and flexibility when utilizing private charter or a fractional share, but where the mission necessitates the back-up option, they will appreciate its availability, ultimately.

    Previously, I’ve devised a scoring system to rank how important a flight is. The highest scoring (most critical) are always backed-up with a supplemental lift solution right up until the flight departs. 

    Securing this level of back-up inevitably requires a higher cost commitment, but on the occasions when missing a critical meeting could have consequences outweighing that cost, the charter backup is justified. 

    Tips When Prioritizing Demand for the Company Jet

    Aside from aircraft unavailability due to technical/maintenance issues, where multiple users have access to the same aircraft, competing demand can occur from different parties.

    In a corporate setting, managing this is best accomplished by developing Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) that contain a checklist of who has priority to fly. This list should explicitly specify a pecking order, clearly stating the alternative methods of transportation (charter, re-scheduling, or airlines) to those lower down the priority chain.

    Making sure that all with access to the jet, as well as their respective assistants, are well-aware of how their transportation logistics will be handled, conflicts and disappointment can be avoided.

    Similarly, a recent emerging trend sees companies and wealthy families preferring not to allow primary executives or family members to travel together, preserving the company leadership or chain of family succession in the event of an accident.

    As a result, I sometimes schedule the aircraft for multiple same-route trips, splitting people between flights to divide the critical passengers. Occasionally we hire supplemental lift, and I always attempt not to have everyone airborne at the same time, preferring to stagger and dilute the risk.

    Many flight departments have well defined ‘no-fly together’ guidelines, which clearly define who stays behind when someone else is gone from the home or headquarters.

    This trend is a clear sign that safety in flight operations is evolving. Although such trends add more complexity to our jobs, it makes good sense and raises the bar on safety.

    It is vital to us all to keep ahead of the curves, clearly defining them in our SOPs, and preserving clear lines to manage passenger expectations versus reality.
